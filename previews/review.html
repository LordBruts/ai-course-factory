Please read this first: the evidence review flagged this topic

There is enough high- and medium-relevance material to support a practical introductory course on photobiomodulation for exercise recovery. However, the evidence is mixed and highly protocol-dependent, with both positive athlete-oriented trials and a human study showing no acute mitochondrial effect, so the course should be positioned cautiously and emphasis placed on limitations and implementation boundaries.

  • Protocols are highly variable; inappropriate device settings may reduce effectiveness or create confusion about expected outcomes.
  • Some sources involve non-standard modalities or combinations (e.g. PBM + osteopathic manipulation, PBMT-sMF, ILIB) that may be outside standard fitness-wellness scope.
  • Avoid presenting PBM as a replacement for medical evaluation or treatment of injury, pain, asthma, or other disease conditions.
  • Do not imply injury diagnosis or therapeutic claims beyond the evidence base.

Introduction
  • In this lesson, you will learn what photobiomodulation is, what recovery research reports, where findings are mixed, and how to discuss red light services clearly, safely, and within scope. (no source)
Slide 1: What PBM is
  • Photobiomodulation, or PBM, uses red or near-infrared light applied to body tissues in a non-invasive way [1, 2].
  • In sport and recovery research, it is studied as an adjunct rather than a standalone answer [1, 2].
  • Studies use lasers, LEDs, and wearable devices [3, 4, 5].
Slide 2: How it is thought to work
  • Reviews propose that PBM may influence cellular bioenergetics, inflammatory signaling, and tissue microenvironments [1, 2].
  • But research methods vary widely, especially treatment parameters and outcome measures [1, 2].
  • One human study found a single PBM session did not increase mitochondrial respiration in skin or skeletal muscle [6].
Slide 3: What recovery evidence shows
  • Across reviews, PBM shows promise for exertion recovery, muscle strength, endurance, and reduced fatigue, but the evidence is not consistent [1, 2].
  • Some studies report better delayed recovery markers or performance after exercise stress [3, 7].
  • Negative or equivocal findings also appear, especially in some trained or low-stress cohorts [2, 6].
Slide 4: Examples from active populations
  • In CrossFit athletes, PBMT-sMF did not beat other recovery options for jump performance at one hour after the workout [7].
  • It did outperform passive recovery on some secondary outcomes and improved jump performance at 24 and 48 hours [7].
  • A combined PBM plus osteopathic study reported exploratory recovery patterns, not clear strength gains [3].
Slide 5: Safety, limits and scope
  • Studies use different devices, wavelengths, timing, and combinations, which makes outcomes harder to predict [1, 2].
  • Some research involves osteopathic manipulation, static magnetic fields, or transcranial use, which may sit outside standard fitness-wellness scope [3, 7, 8].
  • Some studies reported no adverse events or only mild warmth [9, 10].
Slide 6: How to discuss PBM honestly
  • PBM is a recovery service with mixed evidence, and results may depend on the protocol used in research [1, 2].
  • When someone has injury, pain, asthma, or other medical concerns, refer appropriately [4, 9, 10].
Key takeaways
  • PBM may support some recovery outcomes, but results are mixed and protocol-dependent [1, 2, 7].
