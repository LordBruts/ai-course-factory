**How to record**
- **Framing.** Landscape 16:9, 1080p or higher, 25 or 30 fps. Camera at eye level, about
  1–1.5 m away, framed chest-up. Place yourself on the **right third** of the frame and leave
  the left side clear: the study cards and infographics will sit there.
- **Background and light.** Plain, uncluttered background. Soft light from the front, and no
  window behind you.
- **Sound.** Use a lavalier or USB mic if you have one. Record **10 seconds of silence**
  first, for room tone.
- **Delivery.** Read the lines exactly as written; the edit is checked word for word
  against the approved script.
  - Don't read the section titles in brackets.
  - Pause about 2 seconds between sections.
  - If you stumble, pause, then **repeat the whole sentence**. The editor keeps the best take
    and cuts the rest, and never cuts inside a word.
- **Tone.** Real and punchy: confident and conversational, as if briefing a colleague. Lean on
  the words in **bold**.
- **Numbers.** Say "six hundred and fifty nanometres", "twenty-four and forty-eight hours",
  "eight hundred and eight nanometre laser". "PBMT-sMF" is read "P-B-M-T, S-M-F".
- **Length.** About 2 min 40 s.

**Script**

[Opening]
Welcome.
In this course, you will learn **what red light therapy is**, what exercise recovery research reports, **why protocols are hard to compare**, and how to discuss this service **honestly and within scope**.

[What PBM is]
Photobiomodulation, or **PBM**, uses red or near-infrared light applied to the body.
Reviews describe proposed effects on cellular metabolism, oxidative stress, and inflammatory signalling, **but** protocol differences make simple explanations difficult.
Lasers and LED devices also **differ technically**.

[How it may work]
PBM is often described as influencing bioenergetics and immune or oxidative stress pathways.
**But** one human study found a single PBM session **did not increase** mitochondrial respiration in skeletal muscle or skin tissue.
That means mechanism claims should **stay cautious**.

[Recovery and performance evidence]
A review reports **promising effects** for exertion recovery, including muscle strength, endurance, and reduced fatigue in sport performance studies.
In CrossFit athletes, PBMT-sMF **did not beat** other recovery methods for jump performance at one hour, but showed **better jump results** than passive recovery at 24 and 48 hours on secondary analysis.

[Pain and related outcomes]
Evidence on pain is **mixed** across conditions.
A fibromyalgia review reported consistent short-term pain and quality-of-life improvements, though heterogeneity limited generalizability.
In chronic trapezius myofascial pain, 808 nanometre laser was **not superior to placebo** for pain outcomes.

[Protocols and device choices]
Protocol design **matters** because studies vary in wavelength, energy dose, timing, and application target.
Examples in the brief include 650 nanometre LED use before exercise-induced muscle damage and 630 plus 850 nanometre LED use in low back pain research.
Transcranial protocols are **preliminary** and not routine fitness-business practice.

[Safety, scope, and client talk]
Some included trials reported no adverse events or only mild and infrequent adverse effects, but safety reporting is **not uniform** across the literature.
**Stay within scope.**
Present PBM as a **wellness or recovery service**, not a medical treatment.
Be clear that evidence is promising in places, mixed in others, and **not a guarantee**.

[Key takeaways]
The main message is simple.
PBM **may** support recovery in some contexts, but the evidence is **not uniform**.
Your role is to **understand the protocol, stay in scope, and communicate uncertainty clearly**.
